Where did Australia's tech jobs go?

Job ads for ICT professionals (developers, programmers, analysts and security specialists) have fallen to less than half of their January 2019 level. This page shows where it happened, how fast, and how tech compares with the rest of the job market.

−49% ICT job ads in August 2026 compared with January 2019
−61% below the April 2022 peak of about 14,800 ads a month
1 of 48 ICT had the biggest fall of any of 48 occupation groups

Good to know

  • Job ads are the online job advertisements counted each month by Jobs and Skills Australia. They show how much employers want to hire, not how many people already work in a job.
  • ICT Professionals is the official occupation group for software and web developers, programmers, analysts, and database, network and security specialists.
  • Share of job ads means ICT ads as a percentage of all job ads in the same place, which lets us compare big and small states fairly.

1. The boom and the bust

Tech hiring has always moved in waves. Ads hit their highest point in the 20-year record in February 2008, dropped sharply during the 2020 COVID-19 lockdowns, then surged to a post-2019 high in April 2022. Since then they have fallen steadily. In August 2026 there were about 5,800 ICT job ads a month, roughly 61% below that peak. In the chart below, each point on the line is one month, so the higher the line, the more ICT jobs employers were advertising. The labelled dots mark the two peaks, the COVID-19 low and the latest month.

−61%from the April 2022 peak to August 2026

What stands out

The 2022 rebound was big. Ads roughly doubled from the COVID-19 low of about 7,000 in June 2020 to nearly 14,800 in April 2022, which was about 3,400 ads a month above the January 2019 level.

Then the decline began. Ads have now fallen for more than four years, by about 170 ads a month on average. The latest month, about 5,800, is lower than the COVID-19 low and is the lowest of the whole record back to 2006, less than a third of the February 2008 peak of about 19,200.

Was this just a weak job market? No. Counting all other occupations together, job ads in August 2026 were about 22% higher than in January 2019. ICT ads started level with them, rose just as fast in 2021–22, then fell away from mid-2022 onwards and ended 49% below where they began. Both lines in the chart below start at 100, the number of ads in January 2019. A line above 100 means more ads than then, and a line below 100 means fewer.

−49%ICT job ads since January 2019, against +22% for all other jobs

What stands out

The two lines move together until 2022. Both climbed through the recovery, but ads for all other occupations rose further, to about 163 in April 2022 against 129 for ICT.

After that they split. Other occupations eased back and now sit about 22% above their 2019 level, while ICT kept falling to 51. The gap between the lines is about 70 points, so the slump is specific to tech and not a sign of a weak job market in general. ICT ads are now about half their pre-pandemic level, while ads for everything else are about a fifth higher.

2. A nationwide slump, but not an even one

ICT roles make up a smaller slice of all job ads in every state than they did in January 2019. The ACT stands out: about one in ten of its job ads (10.3%) is still for an ICT professional, far above New South Wales and Victoria (about 3.2% and 3.3%) and well above every other state. On the map below each state is shaded by the share of its job ads that are for ICT professionals. A darker colour means ICT is a bigger part of the local job market, and the numbers on the map give the exact share.

10.3%of ACT job ads are for ICT, the highest share in Australia

What stands out

The ACT (red ring) is the only place in the darkest group, at 10.3%. That is about three times New South Wales and Victoria (3.2% and 3.3%) and about eight times the Northern Territory (1.3%).

Big states are not necessarily tech-heavy. Queensland (1.9%) and Western Australia (1.6%) are both under 2%, even though each has far more people than the ACT.

Every share is lower than in January 2019, when the ACT's was 15.8% and New South Wales's was 7.1%.

The biggest cities, where most tech work is advertised, lost the most. Sydney is down 53% since January 2019, Melbourne 48% and Canberra 45%. Hobart is the only capital with more ICT ads than in 2019 (+6%), but that comes from a very small base of about 36 ads a month. In the map below the size of each circle shows how many ICT ads a city has today, and the colour shows how much that number has changed since January 2019. Dark red is a big fall, pale is little change and blue is growth.

−53%Sydney, the biggest tech job market, since January 2019

What stands out

Sydney (about 1,970 ads a month) and Melbourne (about 1,520) are the biggest circles and also among the darkest, so the largest tech markets lost the most. Sydney alone advertises about 2,200 fewer ICT jobs a month than in 2019, and Sydney and Melbourne together about 3,550 fewer.

Brisbane and Perth sit in the middle, down 36% and 29%. Adelaide (−14%) and Darwin (−6%) held up best among the cities that fell.

Hobart is the only city that grew (+6%), but that is from about 34 ads a month to about 36, so it is a tiny change in a tiny market.

In 2019 the ACT advertised more ICT jobs than Western Australia or South Australia, and it still does (about 460 ads a month against 440 and 300). The ACT had 47% fewer than before; Tasmania (+8%) and the Northern Territory (+1%) are the only places not down, but both advertise only about 48 and 35 ICT jobs a month. In the cartogram below each circle is a state, drawn on a grey map of Australia and nudged slightly so the circles do not overlap. The dashed ring shows how many ICT ads there were in January 2019 and the filled circle shows the number now, so the gap between them is the loss.

~460ICT ads a month in the ACT, more than Western Australia (~440)

What stands out

New South Wales and Victoria have the biggest rings, and they have lost the most ads. New South Wales went from about 4,650 a month to about 2,030, and Victoria from about 3,250 to about 1,530.

The ACT is small on a normal map but large here. At about 456 ads a month it is still ahead of Western Australia (about 440) and South Australia (about 300), and it has lost 47% since 2019.

Tasmania and the Northern Territory are tiny, with the ring and the circle almost the same size. A change of a few ads makes them look like they grew.

The lines below show how each state got there. Most climb through the 2021–22 boom and then sink below where they began. NT and Tasmania jump around because their numbers are small. A line below the dashed line means fewer ads than then.

What stands out

The lines rise and fall together up to 2022, then split. New South Wales (−56%), Victoria (−53%) and the ACT (−47%) end below 55, the deepest falls among the larger states.

Queensland (−39%), Western Australia (−34%) and South Australia (−24%) fell less, but are still well below where they started.

Tasmania and the Northern Territory end slightly above 100 (+8% and +1%), but their lines are jumpy because they advertise only about 48 and 35 ICT jobs a month. A handful of ads moves the line a long way.

Not every state lost the same amount, so the map of where tech jobs are advertised has shifted a little. In January 2019, New South Wales had 41% of all ICT job ads across the eight states. Today it has 35%, while Queensland, Western Australia and South Australia each hold a bigger slice than before. In the bars below, each bar is all of the ICT job ads in the eight states, split into slices by state. What stands out

New South Wales and Victoria are still the biggest slices, but together they went from 69% to 61% of all ICT ads.

Every other state gained a bigger slice: Queensland from 14% to 17%, South Australia from 3% to 5%, Western Australia from 6% to 8%, and the ACT edged up from 7% to 8%. Their ads fell too, just by less than New South Wales and Victoria, so their slice of a smaller total got bigger.

3. Tech compared with the rest of the job market

Most kinds of work are advertised more than before the pandemic. Only 11 of the 48 occupation groups have fewer ads than in January 2019, and ICT fell more than twice as far as the next worst (Transport and Design Professionals, and Architects, −23%). Medical practitioners and nurses more than doubled (+123%). In the chart below each row is one group of jobs. A dot to the right of the vertical line means more ads than in January 2019 and a dot to the left means fewer, and the longer the line, the bigger the change.

1 of 48occupation groups: ICT fell the most

What stands out

Almost everything sits to the right: 37 of the 48 groups advertise more jobs than in January 2019.

ICT (red, bottom) is the only group below −25%. Its fall of 49% is more than twice that of the next worst, Transport and Design Professionals, and Architects (−23%).

Only three groups more than doubled: Medical Practitioners and Nurses (+123%), Jewellers, Arts and Other Trades Workers (+107%) and Cleaners and Laundry Workers (+102%).

The fall is not just a story about small occupations. In January 2019 ICT was the second-biggest of the 48 groups, with about 11,400 ads a month. Of the 14 groups that advertised 5,000 or more jobs a month then, half have grown since. The biggest group of all, General-Inquiry Clerks and Call Centre Workers, is almost unchanged (−2%). In the chart below each dot is one group of jobs. The further right, the more job ads the group had in January 2019, and the higher up, the more it has grown since. Dots below the black line have fewer ads than in 2019.

2nd of 48ICT's rank by job ads in January 2019, before the biggest fall of any group

What stands out

ICT (red) is alone in the bottom right: a big group with the deepest fall. In January 2019 it advertised about 11,400 jobs a month, second only to General-Inquiry Clerks, Call Centre Workers and Receptionists (about 13,500).

The next-worst of the large groups, Business, Finance and Human Resource Professionals (about 9,800 ads), is down 21%, less than half as far.

Seven of the 14 groups with 5,000 or more ads in January 2019 have grown since, by between 10% and 123%. ICT is the only one of the 14 that has lost nearly half.

4. Where tech is still a big slice

Tech's share of job ads is lower in every state than it was in 2019, and it has fallen steadily in most. The ACT peaked at 19.0% in 2020 and was still 10.9% in 2026. New South Wales and Victoria went from around 6–7% of ads in 2019–21 to 3.5% in 2026. In the heatmap below each row is a state and each column a year. The darker the cell, the larger the ICT share of that state's job ads, and the number in the cell gives the exact percentage.

19.0%the ACT's peak ICT share of job ads, in 2020

What stands out

The ACT row stays dark throughout, from 16.3% in 2019 to a peak of 19.0% in 2020 and 10.9% in 2026.

The rows for New South Wales, Victoria and Western Australia fade from dark to light. New South Wales goes from 7.4% to 3.5%, Victoria from 6.7% to 3.5% and Western Australia from 4.3% to 1.6%, so the shift happens everywhere and not just in one place.

The timing matters too. Across Australia the share was 6.5% in 2019 and still 5.7% in 2021, then 3.8% in 2023 and 2.9% in 2026, so most of the drop came after 2021.

Over the full 20-year record, the ACT has had the highest ICT share of any state in every one of the 248 months. That lead is shrinking: its share has dropped from a peak of 24.4% in August 2006 to 10.3% now. Across Australia, ICT's share of all job ads is now 2.7%, the lowest of the whole record. In the chart below each line is one state or territory. The higher the line, the bigger the part of that place's job ads that are for ICT. The red line is the ACT and the grey lines are the other seven.

248 of 248months in which the ACT had the highest ICT share of any state

What stands out

The red line is far above all the others at every point, but it has fallen the furthest in percentage points, from 24.4% in August 2006 to 10.3% now, a drop of about 14 points.

The grey lines bunch together at the bottom, between 1.3% and 3.5% now, and most of them are lower now than they were in 2019.

Across Australia, ICT's share of job ads is now 2.7%. That is the lowest of the whole record and well below the high of 7.3% in November 2010.

The capital cities tell the same story. Every city's ICT share is lower than in January 2019. Canberra is still far ahead (10.3%), though it started from about 16%. Sydney's share almost halved, from 8.3% to 4.5%. In the chart below each row is a city. The grey dot is where its ICT share was in January 2019 and the red dot is where it is now, so the line between them shows the drop.

16.4% → 10.3%Canberra's ICT share, January 2019 to August 2026

What stands out

Every red dot is to the left of its grey dot, so every city fell. Canberra has the longest line, from 16.4% to 10.3%, a drop of about 6 points, and it is still far ahead of every other city.

In relative terms Perth fell the most, from 5.4% to 2.0%, which is a fall of almost two-thirds. Sydney's share fell by 45%, from 8.3% to 4.5%.

The shortest lines belong to Hobart (3.2% to 2.3%) and Darwin (3.0% to 1.8%), which started from low shares.

5. Fewer ads, but the tech workforce kept growing

Fewer job ads do not mean fewer tech workers. The 2021 Census counted over 260,000 ICT professionals and 74,000 ICT managers, about 86,000 more than in 2016. Every ICT occupation grew, led by ICT Support and Test Engineers (+54%). Job ads measure new hiring, so hiring can cool even while a large workforce stays in place. The Census is the most recent official count of workers by occupation. In the chart below each bar is one ICT occupation. The longer the bar, the more the number of workers grew between the 2016 and 2021 Censuses.

+54%growth in ICT Support and Test Engineers, 2016 to 2021

What stands out

Software and Applications Programmers are the biggest group, at about 117,000 workers in 2021, up 47%.

ICT Managers grew the least (+26%) but are the second-largest group, at about 74,500 workers, up from about 59,100 in 2016.

The Census counts workers in 2021, before the ad slump, so it cannot show what has happened to the workforce since.

6. Employers also say fewer tech jobs are hard to fill

Job ads are not the only signal. Each year, Jobs and Skills Australia rates occupations as in "shortage" when employers cannot find enough qualified people. Of the 43 ICT occupations on the list, 23 were rated in shortage in 2022. By 2026 that had fallen to 8. The jobs still in shortage are specialist ones. Cloud Engineer, DevOps Engineer, Cyber Security Architect and Cyber Governance, Risk and Compliance Specialist have been rated in shortage in all six years. Each row in the grid below is an ICT job and each column is a year. A red square means the job was rated in national shortage that year, so employers struggled to find enough qualified people, and grey means no shortage.

23 → 8of 43 ICT occupations rated in shortage, 2022 to 2026

What stands out

The red area shrinks from left to right. In 2022, 23 of the 43 ICT occupations (more than half) were in shortage. Software Engineer was rated in shortage from 2021 to 2024 but not in 2025 or 2026.

The 8 jobs in shortage in 2026 are specialists: Cloud Engineer, DevOps Engineer, Data Architect, Data Engineer and four cyber security roles (including Penetration Tester).

So fewer job ads has not made every tech job easy to fill. The list does not say why.

What it adds up to

Tech hiring has cooled sharply since its 2022 peak. It fell further than any other occupation group, in nearly every state and capital city, while the wider job market stayed strong. Fewer ICT jobs are rated in shortage than in 2022, though specialist roles in cyber security, cloud and data are still hard to fill. The data cannot tell us why. It shows that employers advertise far fewer tech roles than they did before the pandemic. Whether this is a temporary reset or a longer-term change is the question to watch.
